Knowledge Base & Statutory Compliance

Frequently Asked Questions

Exhaustive, authoritative guidance on global remote work taxation, statutory thresholds, residency tests, expat incentive regimes, and cross-border payroll compliance for 2026.

1. How does the 183-day statutory tax residency rule work globally?

The 183-day test is the primary statutory baseline for establishing tax residency across the majority of OECD and non-OECD nations. Under this rule, spending more than 183 days within a fiscal year (or any consecutive 12-month period in jurisdictions like the UK and Australia) triggers unlimited worldwide tax residency. Once deemed a tax resident, you become legally obligated to declare and pay tax on your global income (including foreign salary, dividends, capital gains, and rental income) to the host nation.

However, the 183-day test is not the sole residency trigger. Tax agencies routinely assess qualitative factors including the availability of a permanent dwelling, local familial ties, and your center of vital economic interests.

2. What are the "Center of Vital Interests" and "Permanent Home" tests?

Under Article 4 of the OECD Model Tax Convention, tax residency does not rely exclusively on physical calendar day counting. Even if you spend fewer than 183 days in a country, revenue authorities can classify you as a tax resident if they establish that your Center of Vital Interests resides within their borders.

Key indicators evaluated include: where your spouse and school-aged children live, where your primary bank accounts and corporate entities are registered, where you own real estate, and where you maintain social and professional memberships. In situations where two nations both claim you as a resident, bilateral Double Taxation Treaties deploy a strict tie-breaker cascade: 1) Permanent home; 2) Center of vital interests; 3) Habitual abode; 4) Nationality; and 5) Mutual Agreement Procedure between governments.

3. Does obtaining a Digital Nomad Visa automatically make you tax-exempt?

No. A visa or residency permit grants the legal administrative right to live and perform remote work inside a host country without violating immigration laws, but does not dictate your domestic tax liability. Tax obligations are governed independently by domestic tax codes and international treaties.

While select destinations provide specific statutory tax concessions (such as Spain's 24% Beckham Law rate for Digital Nomad Visa holders or Dubai's 0% personal tax regime), other jurisdictions (such as Germany, Portugal under standard IRS, or Greece without statutory approval) tax remote workers under ordinary progressive income tax brackets ranging from 14% to 48% once statutory residency is triggered.

4. What is the difference between a B2B Independent Contractor and an EOR Employee?

The distinction between independent contracting and formal employment is critical for cross-border payroll compliance:

  • B2B Independent Contractor: You operate as a self-employed business entity (e.g. Autónomo in Spain, Partita IVA in Italy, Sole Trader in the UK). You invoice foreign clients directly, manage your own quarterly VAT filings and advance income tax installments, and pay mandatory self-employed social security quotas. You are entitled to deduct legitimate business overheads (equipment, software, workspace).
  • Employer of Record (EOR) Employee: A licensed third-party entity (e.g. Remote, Deel, Oyster) acts as your formal legal employer within your country of residence. The EOR manages statutory local payroll, withholds income tax and employee social security contributions at source, and remits mandatory employer social charges, while providing full statutory employment rights (paid leave, severance, unemployment protection).
5. How does Spain's Beckham Law (Article 93 IRPF) operate for remote workers in 2026?

Spain's Special Expatriate Tax Regime, codified in Article 93 of the Personal Income Tax (IRPF) Act and commonly known as the Beckham Law, allows qualifying professionals moving to Spain to be taxed as non-residents on Spanish-source earned income at a flat 24% rate up to €600,000 (and 47% on income exceeding €600,000) for the tax year of relocation plus five subsequent tax years.

Following the passage of the Spanish Startup Act (Ley de Startups), eligibility has been expanded to international remote workers holding the Spanish Digital Nomad Visa, highly skilled technical talent, and company directors holding up to 20% equity (uncapped for certified startups). Crucially, qualifying individuals must not have been tax residents in Spain during the five preceding tax years.

6. What is Portugal's IFICI regime (NHR 2.0) and who qualifies?

Following the termination of the classic Non-Habitual Resident (NHR) scheme, Portugal introduced the IFICI (Incentivo Fiscal à Investigação Científica e Inovação) regime, frequently called NHR 2.0. Under the IFICI framework, qualifying professionals enjoy a flat 20% IRS income tax rate on Portuguese-source employment and professional freelance income for a period of 10 consecutive years.

Eligibility is strictly restricted to high-value strategic roles, including: university professors and scientific researchers; personnel engaged in certified R&D projects; founders and employees of qualifying innovative tech startups; and specialists in certified economic and industrial development initiatives. Standard digital nomads and remote contractors who do not meet these targeted innovation criteria are subject to standard progressive IRS scales ranging from 13.0% to 48.0%.

7. How does Italy's Regime Impatriati operate under the 2024–2026 reforms?

Under Article 5 of Legislative Decree 209/2023, high-skilled remote employees and professionals relocating their tax residency to Italy benefit from a 50% exemption on personal income tax on employment and self-employment earnings, up to an annual gross ceiling of €600,000. This effective 50% discount reduces the effective tax burden to approximately 11% to 23% depending on income brackets.

If the relocated professional moves to Italy with a minor child or has a child during the residency period, the statutory exemption increases to 60%. To qualify, applicants must possess high qualification credentials, must not have been tax resident in Italy during the three preceding tax years, and must commit to maintaining Italian tax residency for at least four or five consecutive years.

8. How does Dubai / UAE 0% personal income tax work for remote contractors?

The United Arab Emirates imposes 0% personal income tax, 0% personal capital gains tax, and 0% personal social security withholdings on individuals living and working in Dubai under the Virtual Working Programme or operating via a designated Free Zone corporate license. While the UAE introduced a 9% federal corporate tax on net corporate profits exceeding AED 375,000 (~$102,000 USD), personal salaries paid to remote employees and qualifying freelance income remain entirely exempt from domestic taxation.

However, relocating to Dubai does not eliminate home country tax obligations for citizens of countries enforcing citizenship-based taxation (such as the United States) or individuals failing to formally sever tax residency from high-tax European jurisdictions.

9. How does Thailand's DTV Visa tax foreign remittance income?

Thailand's Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) grants digital nomads and remote workers legal residence for up to 180 days per entry, extendable up to 5 years. Tax liability in Thailand is governed by the Thai Revenue Code's 180-day physical presence rule.

Under Thai Revenue Department rulings issued in 2024 (Departmental Instruction No. Paw. 161/2566 and Paw. 162/2566), individuals residing in Thailand for 180 days or more in a calendar year are classified as tax residents. Tax residents bringing foreign-sourced employment or business income into Thailand within the same or subsequent calendar years are subject to Thai personal income tax under progressive brackets from 0% to 35%, unless protected by an applicable bilateral Double Tax Treaty or derived from savings earned prior to Thai residency.

10. What is Permanent Establishment (PE) corporate tax risk for employers?

Permanent Establishment (PE) risk is one of the most critical legal hazards for multinational remote employment. Under domestic corporate tax codes and Article 5 of the OECD Model Tax Convention, when a remote employee routinely works from a foreign country, their regular home office may be characterized as a "fixed place of business" or a "dependent agent PE" for their overseas employer.

If an employee holds decision-making authority, negotiates commercial contracts, or occupies executive managerial standing while operating from a foreign jurisdiction, local tax authorities can assert jurisdiction to tax a portion of the foreign employer's global corporate revenue in that host country. To mitigate PE risk, companies commonly mandate that cross-border personnel operate via an Employer of Record (EOR) or through arm's-length B2B independent contractor agreements.

11. How do Bilateral Social Security Totalization Agreements and A1 certificates work?

Bilateral Social Security Totalization Agreements and European Union Regulation (EC) No 883/2004 are international treaties designed to prevent dual social security taxation on the same cross-border earnings and ensure seamless retirement pension credit accumulation.

When an employee is temporarily posted to another country by their employer, they can apply for an A1 Portable Document (within the EU/EEA and Switzerland) or a Certificate of Coverage (under bilateral treaties between nations like the US, Canada, the UK, and Japan). This official certificate certifies that the worker remains subject exclusively to their home nation's social insurance system, exempting both the worker and their employer from mandatory host nation social security contributions.

12. How are US citizens and Green Card holders taxed when living abroad as digital nomads?

The United States is one of only two nations enforcing citizenship-based taxation (CBT). All US citizens and permanent residents (Green Card holders) are legally obligated to file an annual federal tax return (IRS Form 1040) declaring their worldwide income, regardless of where they live or pay foreign taxes.

To avoid onerous double taxation, US expats leverage two primary statutory mechanisms:

  • Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE - Form 2555): Allows eligible expats who satisfy either the Physical Presence Test (330 full days outside the US in a 365-day window) or the Bona Fide Residence Test to exclude up to $126,500 (for the 2026 tax year) of foreign-earned compensation from US federal income taxation.
  • Foreign Tax Credit (FTC - Form 1116): Provides a dollar-for-dollar credit against US tax liabilities for income taxes paid to a foreign government, which is often preferable for individuals residing in high-tax European jurisdictions.

US expats must also file FinCEN Form 114 (FBAR) if aggregate foreign bank balances exceed $10,000 at any point during the calendar year, and IRS Form 8938 under FATCA rules.

13. How do Double Taxation Treaties (DTT) resolve dual tax residency?

When an individual meets domestic residency criteria in two separate nations simultaneously (e.g., maintaining a home in the UK while spending 190 days in Spain), bilateral Double Taxation Treaties (DTTs) provide binding tie-breaker rules based on Article 4 of the OECD Model Convention.

The tie-breaker evaluates criteria in a strict sequential cascade: 1) The country where the individual has a permanent home available; 2) If homes exist in both, the country with which personal and economic relations are closer (Center of Vital Interests); 3) If undetermined, the country of habitual abode; 4) If habitual abode is in both or neither, the country of nationality/citizenship; and 5) If dual citizens or neither, the tax authorities resolve the dispute via Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP).

14. What are Controlled Foreign Corporation (CFC) rules?

Controlled Foreign Corporation (CFC) rules are statutory anti-avoidance measures enacted by OECD nations to prevent domestic tax residents from deferring personal taxation by accumulating profits in offshore corporate entities (such as an LLC in Wyoming, an OÜ in Estonia, or a Free Zone company in Dubai).

Under standard CFC statutes (including the EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive - ATAD), if a tax resident controls more than 50% of a foreign company that lacks physical economic substance, genuine local employees, or commercial infrastructure, the undistributed passive and active earnings of that foreign corporation are imputed directly to the individual resident as personal taxable income in their host country of residence.

15. What is the tax treatment of cryptocurrency and stock options when relocating abroad?

Cryptocurrency and equity compensation require careful cross-border planning:

  • Cryptocurrency & Digital Assets: Taxation ranges from 0% in crypto-friendly destinations like Dubai and Germany (for private crypto held for longer than one calendar year), to progressive capital gains rates reaching 19%–28% in Spain, 20% in the UK, and 26% in Italy. Moving crypto assets between self-custody wallets is non-taxable, but crypto-to-crypto swaps or fiat liquidations trigger capital gains in most jurisdictions.
  • Employee Stock Options & RSUs: Cross-border taxation of equity compensation is apportioned based on the number of workdays spent in each country between the grant date and the vesting date. Relocating mid-vesting cycle creates multi-jurisdictional withholding and tax return reporting requirements across all countries where service was rendered.
16. Can remote freelancers deduct home office expenses in European countries?

Yes, though statutory documentation thresholds vary substantially. In Spain, registered autónomos working from home can deduct a statutory proportion of domestic utilities (water, gas, electricity, internet) calculated as 30% applied to the ratio of home office square footage over total home surface area. In the UK, self-employed contractors can claim HMRC simplified flat-rate home working allowances or exact calculated proportions.

Direct professional overheads—including computer hardware, mobile telecom contracts, domain registrations, cloud hosting, and required SaaS productivity software—are universally 100% tax-deductible across all major European jurisdictions, provided valid formal tax invoices (Facturas / VAT invoices) are maintained.

17. What is an Exit Tax and does departing my country trigger it?

An Exit Tax (or departure tax) is a statutory capital gains tax imposed on the unrealized market appreciation of financial assets when a resident formally terminates tax residency in a country. The intent is to prevent individuals from building asset wealth in a high-tax nation and liquidating in a low-tax haven without paying accrued taxes.

For example, Spain imposes an exit tax under Article 95 bis of the IRPF Act if an individual has been a Spanish tax resident for at least 10 of the prior 15 years and owns shares valued over €4,000,000 (or €1,000,000 if owning a >25% stake). In the United States, Section 877A levies an expatriation tax on "covered expatriates" surrendering citizenship or long-term Green Cards. Digital nomads moving short-term without massive equity portfolios rarely trigger exit taxes.
